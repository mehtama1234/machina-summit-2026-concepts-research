First-Principles Thesis

Physical AI is not just a larger model placed inside a robot. It is a closed-loop system problem: perception, motion, hardware, data, safety, operations, economics, and human workflows must work together in the real world.

The summit is useful because it is mostly practitioners discussing the hard part after demos: deployment, reliability, component constraints, customer value, and long-horizon embodied work.

Concept Route

Humanoid Robotics

A humanoid robot is a machine shaped like a human—two arms, two legs, a torso—designed to work in environments built for human bodies. The key insight is that factories, warehouses, kitchens, and offices all have doorways, stairs, and tools sized for humans. A robot that fits that shape can walk in and use the same space without major remodeling. The power is not the humanoid form itself, but what the form enables: the ability to learn from watching humans work (video, teleoperation, sensor data), because the robot's joint angles and limb lengths match a human's.

Physical AI

Physical AI is when you put artificial intelligence into a real machine that has to act in the actual world—not just think, but touch things, move, and adjust what it's doing based on what happens. A robot arm picking items in a warehouse, an autonomous vehicle driving on a city street, a drone flying through obstacles—these are all physical AI because the model has to constantly see what's happening with sensors (cameras, touch, position), decide what to do, and immediately measure whether it worked.

Scaling Robot Deployment

Scaling robot deployment means taking a robot that works well in one customer's factory and running 50, 100, or more robots across many different factories, all of which look different, have different layouts, and hit different problems. A single robot in a lab works great. But a robot running at Customer A's warehouse handles one layout and one set of problems. Customer B's warehouse has a different layout, different equipment, different safety rules. Customer C wants the robot running faster than you promised. Each customer is a new engineering problem. Scaling means solving those problems fast enough to keep up, training integration engineers who understand the system, and building software (fleet management, diagnostics, updates) so you can manage 50 robots without 50 on-site engineers.

Language To Action

Language-to-action is the problem of taking what a human says in plain words (like 'pick up that can' or 'unpack this box') and making a robot physically do it. The difficulty is that language alone tells you what to do, not how the physics actually works—how things deform, slide, grip, or break when you grab them.

Robot Data And Learning

Robot data is video, sensor logs, and task records that show robots what works and what fails. Imagine a human wearing VR gloves teleoperated a robot arm to pick a box off a shelf—that video is robot data. So is a recording of the same robot failing to pick that same box in a warehouse because the lighting changed, or the box was dusty. Robot data captures the chain: robot sees something → robot acts → something happens → robot records what it learns. This is different from just labeling photos; it's a sequence of 'I saw this, I tried that, and here's what happened next.'

Robot Hardware And Components

Picture a robot at a truck-loading dock trying to move a 50-pound box. It needs actual mechanical parts—motors and joints that can push with real force, sensors like cameras and depth cameras that see the world clearly, compute power (a small computer brain) to make decisions fast, batteries to keep running, and carefully engineered materials and assembly so nothing breaks after a few uses. Hardware is all those embodied parts that let a robot do physical work. An AI algorithm alone is not enough; the robot's body has to be capable.

Robot Business Models

A robot company succeeds by selling operational outcomes—not machines. You're promising to solve a customer's workflow problem: moving items faster in a warehouse, inserting parts more reliably on an assembly line, or catching equipment failures before they halt production. The business model must cover uptime guarantees, field engineers who fix problems on-site, safety approvals, retraining workers, and what happens when the customer's needs change mid-contract.

Robot Safety And Trust

Imagine a humanoid robot working in an oil rig or warehouse. If it malfunctions, slips, or is hacked, it could fall and crush a worker, or reach out and hit someone. Robot safety is the full set of layers—hardware safeguards, software backup systems, monitoring cameras, and secure controls—that prevent those injuries. Trust is the human belief that the robot won't hurt you: workers need to believe the robot has multiple safety backstops, not just one smart brain that could be fooled.

Agentic AI Workflows

Imagine a robot arm in a factory: it picks up a part, tries to place it, and then looks to see if the placement worked. If not, it tries again. Agentic AI workflows are systems where an AI model (like GPT or Claude) controls that loop—it plans the next action, the robot performs it, sensors report back what happened, and the model observes the result to decide whether to continue or revise course. Unlike a chatbot that generates an answer once, an agentic workflow closes the loop by observing outcomes and adapting. The 'agent' is the software that acts, watches, and corrects itself in the real or simulated world.
