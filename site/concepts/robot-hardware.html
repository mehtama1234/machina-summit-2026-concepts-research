Robot Hardware And Components

Picture a robot at a truck-loading dock trying to move a 50-pound box. It needs actual mechanical parts—motors and joints that can push with real force, sensors like cameras and depth cameras that see the world clearly, compute power (a small computer brain) to make decisions fast, batteries to keep running, and carefully engineered materials and assembly so nothing breaks after a few uses. Hardware is all those embodied parts that let a robot do physical work. An AI algorithm alone is not enough; the robot's body has to be capable.

Ordinary Problem

You can buy commodity motors online and assemble robots from kits. But buying cheap parts does not guarantee a working robot. A weak gripper cannot feel what it is holding and cannot adjust grip strength, so it drops delicate items. A low-resolution camera cannot read dials or text, so inspection tasks fail even if the AI is smart. A compute system limited by battery power and heat cannot run perception and decision-making fast enough; by the time the robot decides to move, the moment has passed. The naive belief—that hardware is solved and only software matters—leads teams into dead ends.

Naive Picture

Hardware is becoming a commodity. Robot arms are sold online, actuators can be mail-ordered, QDD drives and motors are off-the-shelf. So hardware is solved, and the remaining work is AI and software.

Why It Fails

Certain components have commoditized (motors, bearings, standard actuators), but this conceals a critical trap. A clever policy cannot overcome weak hardware. Dexterity, force, precision, thermal management, and real-time latency are all bounded by actuators, sensors, and compute. A robot trying to pick a deformable object with a cheap plastic gripper will fail, not because the policy is bad, but because the gripper has no tactile feedback and cannot modulate force. A robot trying to inspect gauges with a low-resolution camera cannot read the dial, no matter how good the AI. And a robot with compute constrained to 5 watts (thermal limit) cannot run inference fast enough to close control loops, even with an optimized model. Hardware and software co-define each other: poor hardware requires a more complex policy; poor policy requires better hardware. You cannot decouple them.

First-Principles Move

Physical tasks have hard limits that no software can overcome. If a task requires lifting 50 pounds, a 10-kilogram-payload arm simply will not do it; the physics does not work. If you need to grasp small objects in a 10-centimeter space with finger dexterity, you need compact, strong actuators—tendons or quasi-direct-drive motors—and a hand design that no off-the-shelf component provides. If the work happens outdoors in bright sun or at temperature extremes, your cameras and sensors must tolerate those conditions. And milliseconds matter: if the delay between sensing and acting grows too large, control loops break and the robot cannot respond in real time. The frontier is not at the edge of what AI algorithms can do, but at the boundary of what affordable, reliable, manufacturable hardware can support. Hardware and software cannot be decoupled; poor hardware forces a more complex AI model, and poor AI design requires better hardware. You must co-design from the task requirements down.

What Breaks Without It

Teams that chase AI and ignore hardware eventually hit a wall. If your perception system uses cameras that saturate in bright outdoor light, AI cannot recover that information. If your gripper reliability is 92% (breaks 1 in 12 cycles), you need a technician on-site to replace fingers weekly, and your total cost of ownership explodes. If your compute budget is 10 W and your AI model needs 30 W to run inference in real time, you cannot close the loop fast enough—by the time the decision comes back, the world has changed. In production, this manifests as: unpredictable failures, high maintenance cost, and inability to scale to dozens or hundreds of deployments. You're constrained not by algorithmic limitations but by the physics of your actuators.

Summit Role

The hardware panel (Cecile Schwarver, Stereolabs; John Corentis, ARM; Robert from Dexterity; Rob Knights, UMA) anchored this insight. Key points: cameras are not commodities (15 years of R&D to match human perception); compute is not commodities (perception-driven and interaction-driven intelligence are still unsolved); hands and grippers are a serious bottleneck (Rodney Brooks called them 'pathetic,' meaning we don't even have language to describe dextrous tasks). Dexterity operates at the frontier—pushing hardware innovation, finding the right form factors, balancing strength vs. compactness. ARM's role is orchestrating sensing-to-actuation latency in milliseconds, requiring real-time CPUs, functional safety, and efficiency under power and thermal constraints.

Worked Example

Dexterity builds robots for truck loading and unloading. The task is genuinely difficult: boxes vary, contents shift, spaces are tight. Humans work this job, but it is ergonomically punishing. Dexterity designed their robot (the Mech) with 8 degrees of freedom per arm, two arms, 30 kg payload, 5.4 m reach, and the ability to work in spaces a human cannot easily navigate. They then built policies to use that hardware. A naive approach would be to buy a cheaper arm (smaller payload, less reach, fewer joints) and rely on AI to compensate. That doesn't work: the hardware is too weak to lift the load, too short to reach the back of the container, too slow to move. Instead, Dexterity co-designed: what payload do customers need (double OSHA minimum, about 50 lbs)? What reach? What speed? Then they built actuators and arm geometry to meet that spec. The policy learned on top of capable hardware. This is not coincidence; Robert explicitly stated: 'there's a bar for feasibility and a bar for superhuman performance. That's what makes customers willing to use your robots.' Weak hardware makes feasibility impossible.

One Level Deeper

The hardware-software co-design appears in four areas: (1) Actuation—QDD (quasi-direct drive) motors solved low-level impedance control, but they generate heat in hands (many degrees of freedom, small space, high power density). Tendon-driven systems offer another path but require different control laws. No one solution fits all tasks. (2) Sensing—cameras achieve human-level resolution but at high power cost; LiDAR is expensive and bulky but robust to lighting. Sensor fusion (RGB + depth + LiDAR) multiplies data bandwidth: one robot can have 10 cameras at 1920x1080, 10+ Gbps per camera. This data must be transferred to onboard compute, processed in parallel, and converted to action in <100 ms. Stereolabs fused with Ouster to offer integrated vision+LiDAR systems. (3) Compute—latency between sensing and actuation is critical. milliseconds matter. ARM orchestrates CPUs across perception (understanding where the robot is), reasoning (what should it do), and actuation (commanding motors). This requires real-time guarantees, functional safety certification, and power efficiency under thermal constraints (heat dissipation is physical; you cannot dissipate 50 W in a 10 W envelope). (4) Form factor—a humanoid biped optimizes for human-like spaces but adds balance complexity and limits payload. A wheeled base allows more compute and battery in the base, improving available power, but is less suitable for stairs or tight spaces. Each choice trades off generality for task-specific advantage.

Failure Boundary

Hardware excellence has limits. If the task fundamentally violates hardware physics—asking a 10 kg arm to lift 50 kg, or a 200 ms latency system to perform a sub-50 ms control task—no policy fixes it. Also, cost scales with capability: a gripper that never fails costs 5–10 times as much as one with 95% reliability. At some point, the customer budget is exhausted and the task simply cannot be automated affordably. The boundary appears where reliability becomes expensive and lead times become long. Commodity components (motors, bearings) appear instantly; specialized components (custom hands, tactile sensors, high-grade compute modules certified for safety) take 6–18 months to procure. If your robot needs 5 novel hand designs to cover a task space, you're now 2–3 years away from deployment. The frontier of robotics is not the frontier of AI, but the frontier of manufacturing capability and cost.
