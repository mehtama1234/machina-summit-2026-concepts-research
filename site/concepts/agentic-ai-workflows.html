Agentic AI Workflows

Imagine a robot arm in a factory: it picks up a part, tries to place it, and then looks to see if the placement worked. If not, it tries again. Agentic AI workflows are systems where an AI model (like GPT or Claude) controls that loop—it plans the next action, the robot performs it, sensors report back what happened, and the model observes the result to decide whether to continue or revise course. Unlike a chatbot that generates an answer once, an agentic workflow closes the loop by observing outcomes and adapting. The 'agent' is the software that acts, watches, and corrects itself in the real or simulated world.

Ordinary Problem

When you give a robot or software system a task, 'reasoning well' is not the same as 'succeeding in the world.' A language model can write perfect instructions, but if the robot never checks whether the instruction actually worked, it will pile up errors. Factory robots hit dropped parts, grippers drift out of alignment, or lighting changes and the camera sees something different. Without feedback, the robot keeps making the same mistake over and over. The system also has no sense of cost (how many retries before we lose money?), time pressure (the assembly line stops if we stall), or safety (can we touch that operator?). So the real problem: how do you build a system that acts, observes the outcome, and learns from failures before they cascade into lost productivity or safety hazards?

Naive Picture

Give a large language model a task description, and it will reason through the steps and produce the answer. More scale and better prompting means better results, and the system gets smarter by itself.

Why It Fails

Agentic workflows in physical systems require feedback loops: plan, act, observe, verify, revise. A robot that predicts the next action but never confirms whether the action worked will pile up compounding errors. Unlike text generation where you read the output once, an agent in a factory must correct its course, retry failed grips, and adapt to a changed environment. The model alone has no notion of cost (how many retries have we burned?), time pressure (the line stops if we stall), or safety constraints (do not touch the operator). An agent without external constraints and monitoring becomes expensive and unreliable.

First-Principles Move

An agentic workflow is a closed loop: the AI model generates a hypothesis (the next action to take), the robot or software executes that action, sensors or code measures what actually happened, the model observes the outcome, and it decides to continue, revise, or retry. The magic is not in how smart the model is at reasoning—it's in how tight and fast the observation-action cycle runs. Robotics teams that scale are those that nail three things: tight observation loops (cameras, force sensors, and real-time dashboards showing what went wrong), simulation fidelity (test policies in simulation before burning expensive hardware), and data curation (capture failures, label exactly what went wrong, and feed those lessons back into retraining). The model alone cannot know what 'success' and 'failure' look like; that understanding must come from the data and feedback loops that humans and engineers design.

What Breaks Without It

Without this lens, teams treat the model as a finish line rather than a starting point. They release a robot that reasons well in isolation but fails in the wild because it cannot observe side effects, correct, or learn from errors in deployment. Companies confuse agent capability (can the model write code?) with agent robustness (can the system recover from mistakes?). The robot degrades in value over months because the model's assumptions drift as real-world data changes, and no feedback loop retrains or monitors it.

Summit Role

This theme runs through the OpenAI/Hugging Face fireside (Laura Modiano, Thomas Wolf) and the Nvidia Inspire project cited by Jim Fan. Laura emphasizes that robotics teams using Codex are building agents across multiple modalities: natural-language control, video-based dashboards for anomaly detection, multi-part training loops where past failures inform new experiment design. Thomas highlights that the best robotics teams are 'super technical'—not just good at ML but deep in actuator firmware, simulation, and data pipeline. The shared insight: Codex/GPT help teams compress months of boilerplate coding into weeks, freeing engineers to focus on the core problem—teaching the agent what good and bad data look like. Orbit (ETH Zurich) built a full command-and-control dashboard for four-arm space robots, where multimodal logs (video, audio, system telemetry) are fed into agents that predict failure modes.

Worked Example

Sunrise manufactures electronic components. They deploy an intelligent robotic cell with two arms, cameras, and a multi-model training pipeline. Day 1: Sunrise visits the customer, captures video of manual labor (30+ hours), trains a vision model to identify part placement, actuator trajectories. The cell is shipped pre-trained; on day one at the customer site, it runs the learned policy and handles 80% of item placements automatically. Day 2-7: The cell collects anomaly data (dropped parts, misaligned grips, timeout retries). Engineers use Codex to auto-generate diagnostic scripts: which failures cluster together? Is it low light, part variation, or gripper drift? The model refines. Week 2: the cell runs 50 parts/hour without operator intervention. Customer asks, 'Can we hit 75 parts/hour?' Normally this would require hardware redesign (weeks). Instead, the team uses Codex to explore retiming grip sequences, optimize dwell times, and instrument sensor feedback. Without the agent loop (observe anomalies → hypothesize cause → test in simulation → deploy update → remeasure), this pivot would take months. With it, tight feedback loops compressed timeline to days.

Illustrative example: A fleet of Samsara vehicles uses a 'coaching agent' that observes driver behavior (harsh braking, wide turns). The agent identifies when a driver needs coaching and logs contextual data (road conditions, time of day, vehicle load). Over a fleet of 10,000 vehicles, the coaching agent surfaces patterns: drivers hired in month 3 of year have 12% more accidents; rainy days on highway route A see 3x more hard-braking events. The agent cannot fix the problem directly, but it arms human managers with signal to retrain specific cohorts or reassign routes.

One Level Deeper

Agentic workflows in robotics succeed when three conditions hold. First, tight observation loops. Robots must close the loop fast: act, measure, decide within seconds or minutes, not hours. This requires dense instrumentation (cameras, IMUs, force sensors) and real-time telemetry pipelines. Thomas Wolf emphasized that data understanding is harder in robotics than LLMs because 'you cannot just read it; you have to watch it.' A dashboard that visualizes what the robot sees, where it failed, and why is not a nice-to-have; it is the core product.

Second, simulation fidelity. You cannot test every policy on a real robot; it is too expensive and risky. Physics-based simulation (Orbit, Genesis) allows fast iteration: train the policy in simulation, measure how often it succeeds on known tasks, then deploy to reality with confidence. But the sim-to-real gap is real (friction models are approximate, lighting changes, parts wear). The agent must adapt when reality diverges from simulation—hence the closed-loop again.

Third, data integration and retention. Robotics companies that scale are those that build systems to capture failure cases, curate datasets, and feed them back into retraining loops. Micro-AGI and others do high-quality data collection (label the exact gripper angle, part pose, outcome) so that subsequent agents can learn from it. Without this, each robot is an island, and you repeat the same discovery errors across deployments.

Failure Boundary

An agentic workflow collapses when observation loops break. Early warning signs: the robot performs well in simulation but fails in the field because sim-to-real gap was not bridged; the team ships agents without monitoring and cannot detect when the model's predictions drift from reality (concept drift); anomalies accumulate silently and the robot begins to fail without any trace of why. You will observe this when field robots require frequent human intervention to recover from stuck states, or when performance degradation is so gradual that managers do not notice until quarters of lost productivity pile up. Another failure mode: the training loop is broken—failures are not captured, curated, or fed back, so the deployed agent learns nothing and degrades as conditions change. By that point, redeployment costs exceed the value of the robot. The sector-level failure point: if agentic robotics systems cause repeated safety incidents (the model attempted an unsafe action and monitoring failed to catch it), or if robots consistently miss their economic targets because the agent requires constant human repriming, then the use case is no longer viable and investment shifts to simpler, more predictable automation.
