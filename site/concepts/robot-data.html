Robot Data And Learning

Robot data is video, sensor logs, and task records that show robots what works and what fails. Imagine a human wearing VR gloves teleoperated a robot arm to pick a box off a shelf—that video is robot data. So is a recording of the same robot failing to pick that same box in a warehouse because the lighting changed, or the box was dusty. Robot data captures the chain: robot sees something → robot acts → something happens → robot records what it learns. This is different from just labeling photos; it's a sequence of 'I saw this, I tried that, and here's what happened next.'

Ordinary Problem

Robots don't have a free source of data the way language models do. Language models had the entire internet written for free. Self-driving cars get data from millions of drivers on the road every day—those drivers collect data for Tesla without being asked. But robots have neither. You can't build a warehouse of robots, switch them on, and let them collect data while working. You have to actively control them (through teleoperation: a person in a VR headset controlling the robot's hand), build fake training worlds in simulation, or hire humans to label videos. Data is expensive and slow to gather.

Naive Picture

Robotics just needs data like language models did. Language models trained on the internet. Autonomous vehicles trained on millions of miles of driving. So robots need millions of hours of teleoperation or field data, and scale will solve the problem.

Why It Fails

Robotics faces a structural data disadvantage. Language models train on the entire internet—trillions of tokens, free at scale, collected passively. Autonomous vehicles collect data from millions of drivers on the road every day, free from the end user. But robotics has no free data source. You cannot buy a warehouse of robots and have them passively collect data while working. You have to actively teleoperate them, generate data through simulation, or hire humans to label it. Steve Xie at Lightwheel calculated that physical AI requires 1 billion continuous learning loops—1,000 times more data than autonomous driving or language models—because robots interact physically with everything (not just predicting, but touching, deforming, grasping, pushing). Today, the largest deployed fleet is Tesla's cars (1 million vehicles), but even that is 0.1% of the data robotics needs. Robot data is scarce, expensive, and slow to collect.

First-Principles Move

Robot data differs from image labels in one hard way: it has to capture cause and effect across time. When you label a photo 'cat' or 'dog,' you're done. But a robot learns from sequences: it sees a gripper approaching a coffee mug, it closes the gripper, the mug either lifts smoothly or the gripper slips and the mug falls. The robot must record all three frames, because the outcome (success or failure) teaches it how hard to squeeze and how fast to move. The data flow is: (1) human demonstrations—a trained operator in VR gloves shows the robot how to grasp, fold, or place objects by doing the task; this video captures human dexterity and diverse ways to solve problems. (2) Simulation trials—run thousands of variations of the same task in a physics engine (MuJoCo, Isaac Gym) where you can vary object size, height, friction, lighting without cost. (3) Real deployment—put the robot in a customer's warehouse or factory; when it fails on something it never saw in simulation (a novel object, a worn gripper), log that failure and have a human teleoperate the solution. Feed that logged solution back into training. This loop—human demo → simulation check → real robot try → learn from failure—is where robot data actually teaches.

What Breaks Without It

Without this structure, teams either oversample cheap data (simulation) and produce brittle models that fail in reality, or they starve for data and plateau in capability. If you treat robot data like image labels—collect a dataset once, train once, ship the model—you build a frozen system that regresses when deployed to new environments. Worse, if you lack deployment data, you never know whether your model actually works until customers report failures. The model trainer becomes decoupled from ground truth. Continuous learning is not optional; it's the only way to maintain improvement after deployment. A robot fleet with no feedback loop learns nothing from its mistakes.

Summit Role

Steve Xie (Lightwheel) made continuous learning the centerpiece of the summit: human demonstrations (egocentric data) + simulation evaluation (Robo Finals benchmark) + deployment stack (Robo Stack) must form a closed loop. Vasudevan Sundarababu (Scientific Robotics) focuses on scaling data operations to support foundation models. The data-gaps panel identified the core tension: every robotics company needs data, but few can generate it fast enough. Xie's insight reframes data not as fuel (consumed, standardized) but as learning (continuously improved, specialized). His three-product stack (Ego Suite, Robo Finals, Robo Stack) operationalizes this: human data feeds evaluation, evaluation guides what gets deployed, deployment feeds the next cycle.

Worked Example

Lightwheel supports foundation models across humanoid companies (Figure, Sanctuary, 1X, AGIBOT) and industrial leaders (Samsung, BYD). A typical robot learns through: (1) human egocentric demonstrations—trained humans wearing VR gloves teleoperate the robot to show how to pick, place, or manipulate objects. These videos are diverse (different hands, different operators, different scenes) and capture the full range of human dexterity. (2) Simulation evaluation—Robo Finals benchmarks the foundation model in 100s of variations of the same task (different object sizes, different heights, different friction). No real robots run; only simulation. This tests generalization without cost. (3) Deployment—the robot is deployed to a real customer site. When it fails on a task variant not seen in simulation, Lightwheel flags that failure and collects teleoperation data to recover. That data feeds back into the next pre-training cycle. One deployment might generate 50 hours of failure cases; the next version of the model learns from those 50 hours. This loop ran across Lightwheel's ecosystem in Q1 2026 with $100M in sales, meaning dozens of robot deployments feeding data back to the platform in real time.

One Level Deeper

The data pyramid is inverted: robot data is tiny (30,000–1 million robots deployed), human egocentric data is abundant (billions of humans), and simulation data is infinite (can generate trillions of trajectories in MuJoCo or Isaac Gym). The challenge is diversity-validity tradeoff. Simulation gives perfect accuracy but low diversity (all data from one simulator). Teleoperation gives diversity but low volume (one human, one robot at a time). Human video gives diversity and volume but is not robot-specific (humans have different kinematics and don't follow robot constraints). The solution is layered: pre-train on human video (scale + diversity), validate in simulation (low-cost early filtering), fine-tune on robot teleoperation (robot-specific grounding), then deploy and learn from failures. Continuous learning means the model sees every anomaly encountered in the field: a novel object, an unusual bin configuration, a worn gripper. Each deployment becomes a data collection event. Over time, the deployed fleet becomes the data farm.

Failure Boundary

This breaks down when deployment feedback is not captured or when simulation is too inaccurate to predict real-world performance. If robots are deployed but no one logs which tasks failed or which new scenarios were encountered, the learning loop closes silently, and the model regresses over time as customers encounter edge cases. Also, sim-to-real gap is real: a simulation that shows 95% success may see only 60% success in the field due to unmeasured friction, cable play, or sensor noise. Continuous learning mitigates but doesn't eliminate this. The scaling limit appears around 10,000–100,000 deployed robots per company: at that scale, the diversity of field data exceeds the diversity of available simulation or human demonstration, and the bottleneck shifts to evaluation velocity (can you run enough simulation trials to rank candidate models before the next deployment?).
