physical-ai and robotics

Physical AI

Physical AI is when you put artificial intelligence into a real machine that has to act in the actual world—not just think, but touch things, move, and adjust what it's doing based on what happens. A robot arm picking items in a warehouse, an autonomous vehicle driving on a city street, a drone flying through obstacles—these are all physical AI because the model has to constantly see what's happening with sensors (cameras, touch, position), decide what to do, and immediately measure whether it worked.

Ordinary Problem

A language model can be wrong and nobody gets hurt—it just gives bad advice. But a physical robot makes mistakes with real consequences. If your warehouse robot misses an object because it calculated the grasp angle wrong by a millimeter, that's money lost and work delayed. If an autonomous vehicle misreads the road, someone could get hurt. Physical systems also can't just 'retry' cheaply: every failure costs time, money, and safety. The robot has to be reliable from the start, and every problem the robot encounters in the real world needs to be fixed quickly—not weeks later when engineers finally analyze the data.

Naive Picture

Physical AI works like language models do: if you scale the model, throw more data at it, and improve the architecture, it will eventually learn to control robots and autonomous systems just as language models learned to predict text. A world model trained on internet video can teach a robot what will happen when it takes an action, so it can plan without needing real-world feedback loops. Simulation is sufficient for validation. The constraints are engineering problems to be solved, not fundamental barriers.

Why It Fails

Physical AI faces three hard constraints that digital AI does not. First, the data subsidy that enabled language models does not exist: there are 300 trillion tokens of human text freely available online, but only 270,000 hours of robotics data exist globally—roughly one human lifetime of experience versus all human written language. Second, the physical world is chaotic. Small measurement errors compound exponentially (the Lyapunov exponent): a robot that measures its hand position wrong by 1 millimeter will be 10 centimeters off two seconds later because perturbations grow exponentially. A world model approximates reality, and approximations break down predictably over time. So no amount of simulation can fully predict physical behavior—continuous measurement and correction are required. Third, failure risk scales with the number of people in the environment. A misprediction in language models causes awkward text; a misprediction in an autonomous vehicle harms people. Regulators require reliability to scale quadratically: an event harming 10 people must be 100 times rarer than one harming 1 person. These constraints mean digital AI happens first.

First-Principles Move

Physical AI is fundamentally a closed loop: the robot sees the world (sensors), decides what to do (model), acts (motors), watches what happened (sensors again), and learns from it. This is completely different from digital AI, which lives entirely in a computer making guesses about text. The key insight from chaos theory is that the real world is chaotic—tiny measurement mistakes grow exponentially. If a robot's hand position is off by 1 millimeter, 2 seconds later it will be off by 10 centimeters or more, because small errors snowball. No amount of simulation can predict this perfectly, so the robot must continuously measure what actually happened and correct itself in real time. The speed of this feedback loop—how fast the robot can see a failure, get new data, retrain, and deploy the fix—directly determines whether the robot survives in production. A company that closes this loop in days beats one that takes weeks. Simulation helps plan ahead, but it is never enough by itself.

What Breaks Without It

If you treat physical AI as just 'digital AI with actuators' and rely primarily on simulation and pre-training, deployments fail catastrophically in the field. The robot works in the lab, but in the real factory it encounters conditions not in the training set: an oily floor (aluminum production), vibrations from nearby machinery, changing lighting, or unexpected objects. The robot slips, drifts, or makes errors. If the feedback loop is slow—if fixing the issue requires weeks of re-training and data labeling—the customer loses confidence and the system is abandoned. Alternatively, teams hand-code fixes for each edge case, creating brittle, unmaintainable systems. Scaling becomes impossible because every new site requires months of customization. The fundamental insight is lost: the robot must be designed as a continuous learner from day one, not as a trained model that runs inference. Without this, physical AI remains stuck in demos and pilots.

Summit Role

Eric Landau (Encord, data layer for physical AI) structures the problem around three equations: scaling laws (why digital AI has a data advantage), chaos theory (why simulation is not sufficient), and societal risk (why failure rates must be lower than in digital systems). Dr. Karolina Parada (Google DeepMind) demonstrates this through Gemini Robotics, a vision-language-action model that learns from human demonstrations and improves over time via reinforcement learning and world models. The Factory Deployment Gap panel shows the operational reality: robots in the real world encounter a 'data paradox'—too much raw data to analyze, but not enough labeled data to train models. The solution is not bigger models but faster feedback loops: automated data pipelines, triage systems that flag anomalies, and site reliability engineers deployed at customer facilities who continuously tune models and collect the right data.

Worked Example

Illustrative: A robotics startup deploys a manipulation system to a warehouse. In the lab, it picks items from one bin and places them in another with 95% success. In the customer's warehouse, success drops to 70%. The team investigates: the warehouse floor is 0.5 degrees tilted (not noticeable to humans). The bin locations shift slightly each day. The lighting varies depending on whether the dock door is open. The robot's grasp detector was trained on brightly lit objects and fails in shadow. The startup has two choices: (1) spend 8 weeks re-training the model on warehouse data, losing the customer's trust, or (2) set up a continuous feedback loop. They choose (2). Every failure is logged with video, sensor traces, and the reason for the failure. Within 3 days, they identify the top 5 failure modes. Within a week, they deploy a new model. Within two weeks, success is back to 92%. But it is not stable at 95% until month two, when the model has seen 1000 unique items and learned the edge cases. The customer understands this is a learning system, not a static product. The data flywheel closes: the more items picked, the more data collected, the better the model.

One Level Deeper

Physical AI systems are fundamentally limited by the speed at which errors can be detected and corrected. In language models, an error is discovered during evaluation (checking the output against a held-out test set) and fixed in the next training run days or weeks later. In physical AI, an error is discovered when the robot fails in the real world, harming throughput or safety. The cost of discovery is high and the cost of correction is urgent.

The solution is to invert the learning architecture. Instead of 'train once, deploy many times,' the paradigm becomes 'deploy continuously, train continuously.' This means (1) every robot is a data collection platform with comprehensive telemetry (video, joint angles, sensor readings, task outcome), (2) failures are triaged automatically (which team should review this—perception, control, grasp planning?), (3) model updates are deployed to the entire fleet incrementally, and (4) the fleet's collective data immediately improves the next version. This is why Encord focuses on data infrastructure, not models. The bottleneck is not training algorithms; it is the pipeline that takes a terabyte of robot video per day, finds the 1% that represents failures or edge cases, and turns it into actionable training data.

Simulation accelerates this loop: a world model trained on fleet data can be used to evaluate proposed policy changes before deployment, avoiding field failures. But simulation is an optimization, not a substitute. The real world will always generate surprises. The practitioner's job is to shorten the surprise-to-fix cycle from months to days.

Failure Boundary

Physical AI fails when the environment exceeds the robot's observability or when the iteration loop becomes too slow to sustain. A robot trained on picking items in a warehouse will fail if deployed to pick items in a freezer (different lighting, frost on sensors), on an oil-stained factory floor (slipping), or during a shift change when the layout reorganizes. The model does not know these environments exist. If the feedback loop is slow (manual data review, slow labeling, training takes weeks), the robot remains broken for too long and the customer cancels the contract. If the feedback loop is fast but the model cannot adapt to the new environment (poor generalization), then hand-coding workarounds becomes necessary, converting the system into a brittle collection of patches. The second boundary is safety: if a physical AI system operates near humans or valuable equipment, failure rates must be incredibly low. A language model can fail 10% of the time and still be useful. A robot in a factory must achieve 99.9% reliability from day one. If the startup cannot close the gap from 85% to 99.9% reliability within the first month of deployment, the customer will not continue, and the unit economics collapse. At this point, practitioners observe that the 'data paradox' is not a solvable engineering problem—it is a fundamental misalignment between how much data the system generates and how much labeled data the team can actually process. The startup folds, or the team discovers they should have been collecting and triaging data from pilot one, not from production one.
