physical-ai and robotics

Language To Action

Language-to-action is the problem of taking what a human says in plain words (like 'pick up that can' or 'unpack this box') and making a robot physically do it. The difficulty is that language alone tells you what to do, not how the physics actually works—how things deform, slide, grip, or break when you grab them.

Ordinary Problem

In a factory or warehouse, humans can pick up a cardboard box wrapped in plastic and pull out what's inside without tearing it. A robot using just language and pattern-matching from videos often fails: it tears the plastic in a way that breaks its gripper, or throws the whole box away, or gets stuck because the plastic didn't move the way the model expected. The factory worker's target is maybe 2 minutes per box. The robot is slower, less reliable, and keeps asking a human to step in.

Naive Picture

A robot can follow instructions if you give it a good language model. If the model understands what you said—'pick up the can' or 'sort these by color'—it should be able to do it. Bigger models, more data, better results.

Why It Fails

Language models learn semantics and reasoning from text, but they cannot learn physics or action from text alone. When you post-train a vision-language-action model on robot teleoperation data, the model inherits the language capability but only learns action from a narrow, limited robot dataset. The result: lab demos that fail in the field when anything varies from the training scenario. Grippers drop objects. Vision mismatches cause wrong paths. The model has no action prior strong enough to handle deformable materials, clear plastic, occluded targets, or broken boxes—all problems common in real work.

First-Principles Move

To make a robot reliable at physical tasks, it must first learn how the world actually moves and changes—what happens when you squeeze plastic, how materials deform, where friction helps and where it fails. The best teacher for this is not a collection of videos showing just one robot doing one task over and over (which is brittle). It is millions of hours of internet video showing all kinds of people, objects, and materials interacting in all kinds of ways. The aim is to pre-train the robot model on this internet-scale video to build an intuitive sense of physics, then fine-tune it with only a modest amount of task-specific recording of a human doing the factory task, connecting that physics knowledge to the robot's actual motors and sensors. Now when the robot hears 'unpack that box,' it invokes physics understanding of how plastic tears and corrugated paper crumples, not brittle pattern-matching.

What Breaks Without It

Without this insight, teams spend years collecting more and more robot teleoperation data, hoping scale will solve brittleness. It won't. Robots trained on vision-language-action models alone may work on 80% of demo tasks but fail catastrophically on variability: different box shapes, different materials, partial occlusion, broken or deformed objects. In factories doing truck loading or box unpacking—the core use cases—this brittleness makes the robot unreliable, requiring human intervention on every third or fourth task. You never achieve full autonomy; you get a system that needs constant babysitting.

Summit Role

Andrew Wooten at Roto AI positioned this as the central challenge: current robots either follow pre-programmed trajectories (no learning) or run vision-language-action models (brittle learning). Carolina Parada (Google DeepMind) showed Gemini's multimodal approach to agentic reasoning; Wooten showed why the architecture matters more than model size. The debate is real: do you build bigger language-based policies, or do you fundamentally change the architecture to learn physics first? Summit talks cluster around this divide—Parada defending VLA scaling, Wooten defending world action models.

Worked Example

Roto AI deployed a robot to unpack bearing boxes at a German automotive factory. The boxes are densely packed, wrapped in clear plastic and corrugated wax paper, and every box is slightly different. Using a vision-language-action model (a leading open-source version), the robot was given 20 hours of teleoperation data. Result: it failed repeatedly, throwing whole boxes away or tearing plastic in ways that would break the robot hand or the product. The customer's cycle time target was 2 minutes per box; the robot wasn't meeting it. Then Roto switched to its world action model (pre-trained on millions of hours of internet video, then fine-tuned with 10 hours of robot data). Same hardware. Same task. The robot unpacked 30 boxes autonomously at 100% success rate, beating the 2-minute cycle time, requiring zero human intervention. The difference: the video pre-training gave the model an implicit understanding of how plastic tears, how corrugated paper crumples, how grasp angles change when a box is slightly tilted. The model learned that in video space, then translated it to robot action.

One Level Deeper

The technical challenge is two-fold. First, video models generate all frames in parallel (noise → all frames at once), which breaks causality: the model draws the final grip position independent of the approach phase. Roto re-trained the video architecture to be causal—each frame depends on the previous one, like a language model's token-by-token generation. This forced the model to respect physical consequence. Second, typical video models take 30 seconds to generate a 2-second clip; robots need real-time closed-loop control (100-300 ms per decision). Roto built a model that runs in 200 ms and is physically accurate enough that the one-to-one mapping from generated video frame to robot joint angles becomes reliable. The policy works by generating a video of the robot doing the task, then translating that video frame-by-frame into low-level motor commands in real time.

Failure Boundary

This approach begins to fail when the task requires world state that video cannot infer. If a bearing is loose inside a box and the robot cannot see it until the moment it touches, video pre-training won't help. Also, real-time inference latency is still a constraint: if closed-loop latency exceeds 300 ms, the robot cannot correct for unexpected contact or slip. And if the robot is asked to do something never seen in internet video—a specialized factory operation or a task involving tools and procedures only a particular company uses—the video prior offers little advantage over a VLA trained on that task directly. The boundary is roughly: general dexterous manipulation in unstructured settings (yes); specialized, invisible-state, or procedurally novel tasks (not yet).
