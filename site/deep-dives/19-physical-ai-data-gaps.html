Talk 19 · Stanford and Centific

Jiajun Wu / Stanford and Vasu Sundarababu / Centific: Physical AI's Data Gaps

When you watch a robot fold clothes or pack a box, it's seeing video but not feeling pressure, weight, or contact—and there's no common way to collect or combine these different sensor streams so robots can learn from real-world mistakes the way humans do.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Imagine a factory worker learns to gripper a fragile part: they feel 60 Newtons of pressure is safe but 80 Newtons breaks it. A robot trained only on video cannot feel that difference. Right now, vision data, touch data, action commands, and results live in separate silos across different companies with no shared standard. When a robot fails, nobody documents why—just that it failed. Without synchronization and explanation, each company rebuilds the same dataset from scratch, and robots cannot learn from failure the way humans do.

First-Principles Model

Physical AI data must be an experience trace: a continuous record that says 'I saw X, I felt Y pressure, I commanded Z torque, and then Q happened.' Video alone is a snapshot. Real learning needs synchronized sight, touch, force, action, and the timestamp of each change. Then crucially: when the robot fails, someone has to write down why (camera was not calibrated, surface was wet, pressure was too high) so the next attempt learns what broke and how to recover. Without that causal structure, more data just adds noise instead of skill.

Key Claims

  • The main bottleneck is high-quality physical data, because physical data is expensive, constrained, and harder to generate than digital data.
  • The data ecosystem is fragmented: egocentric data, manipulation data, imitation data, tactile data, and sensory data lack common schemas and benchmarks.
  • Robotics needs multimodal synchronization across vision, touch, force, actions, proprioception, and time.
  • A failure can be high-quality data if it reveals why a robot failed and how recovery should work.
  • Bad data includes corrupted or uncalibrated collection, such as cameras that prevent accurate reconstruction.
  • Golden datasets should be built around tasks people actually care about, not just tasks a lab robot can already do.

Worked Example

A robotics lab wants to train a grasping model and collects 500 hours of RGB video. They train a model and test it on 100 real tasks. It achieves 60% success. They then add tactile sensors (touch/force), synchronized with the RGB data at 60 Hz. The same 500 hours now becomes synchronized multimodal data: vision + pressure + torque at each joint. The model retrained on this multimodal data reaches 82% success on the same 100 tasks.

But now they encounter a batch of 50 failures. Rather than discard these, they annotate why each failed: "Gripper pressure exceeded 50 Newtons on fragile object," "Camera was uncalibrated, lost 3D position," "Contact surface was wet, friction coefficient dropped." With failure explanations, these 50 failed attempts now carry higher signal than 500 unlabeled successful attempts. They retrain and reach 89%. The learner now understands not just what works, but what breaks and how.

One Level Deeper

Physical AI data is fundamentally an experience trace, not just a passive recording. A useful trace must capture the state before action, the action taken, what was sensed during execution, the outcome, and crucially, whether it succeeded or failed and why. This structure matters because the robot must learn causality—that pressing with 60 Newtons works but 80 Newtons breaks the object. RGB video alone cannot carry this: you cannot infer pressure or force from pixels. The fragmentation Vasu and Jiajun emphasize is that labs collecting vision data, touch data, action data, and evaluation data in separate silos cannot combine them because there is no common schema, no synchronized timestamps, no shared frame of reference.

The golden dataset problem is that datasets must be built for tasks people actually care about, not for tasks the lab robot can already do. And they must be causally annotated by domain experts—roboticists and actual practitioners who know what constitutes success and what physical parameters matter. A badly calibrated camera that prevents accurate 3D reconstruction is bad data even if the video looks fine. A failure with a calibrated multimodal trace and causal explanation is good data because it teaches the model where its prior breaks.

Failure Boundary

This approach fails when the data becomes too expensive to collect at scale. If synchronizing vision, touch, force, proprioception, and language annotations at 60 Hz across 10,000 hours of human egocentric data costs $50 million, the economics break. The field cannot afford to build golden datasets for every task and every embodiment separately.

It also breaks if standardization is not industry-wide. If Company A uses one schema for action data and Company B uses another, robots trained on A's data cannot learn from B's data. Each new task requires rebuilding the benchmark. The inflection point appears when fragmentation costs more than standardization: if engineers spend more time format-converting datasets than training models, the architecture has failed. Without shared benchmarks and evaluation frameworks, field teams cannot compare whether their model truly progresses or merely overfits to their local dataset. A robot that works in one company's factory but fails in another signals that the data and evaluation standards are not capturing the real task structure.

Important Terms

Experience Data

Physical AI data that captures an interaction over time, not just a static image or label.

Multimodal Synchronization

Aligning vision, tactile, force, action, proprioception, audio, and temporal signals so a model can learn cause and effect.

Golden Dataset

A high-signal dataset with relevant tasks, calibrated sensors, expert labels, failure explanations, and evaluation-ready structure.

Failure-as-Signal

The idea that unsuccessful attempts can be valuable when they expose recoverable mistakes and physical causes.

Benchmark Standardization

Shared schemas, task definitions, assets, and metrics that let the field compare and improve models.

Connection To Stanford Agent Learning

  • The panel directly links to Stanford-style benchmark design through task selection, behavior benchmarks, and evaluation data.
  • It sharpens the SFT/RL discussion: demonstrations and failures both matter, but only if the feedback labels explain what happened.
  • It reinforces the idea that self-improvement needs clean feedback channels, not just more rollouts.

Practical Caution

Do not equate data quality with successful demonstrations only. For robotics, a failed attempt with calibrated sensors and causal explanation may be more useful than a clean success clip with missing context.

Takeaway

The data-gap thesis is that robotics needs experience datasets, not just videos. The critical missing layer is standardized, synchronized, causally meaningful feedback across tasks and embodiments.
