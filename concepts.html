Humanoid Robotics

Suppose a factory assembles expensive servers for data centers—each server has up to $350,000 in components inside, and breaking a single GPU costs $30,000 to replace. A human worker learned the assembly process by watching and doing. Now the factory wants a robot to do the same job. The problem is not physics or raw intelligence—it is that the robot has no idea how to work in that specific factory. It does not know the layout, the exact tool positions, or the sequence of motions that fit. Traditional robots are built for one task only, so redesigning one for each factory is impossibly expensive. A humanoid robot solves this differently: if you can feed it video of a human doing the assembly, it can learn that exact sequence and reuse the same space and tools. That is the ordinary problem humanoid robots address.

Physical AI

A language model can be wrong and nobody gets hurt—it just gives bad advice. But a physical robot makes mistakes with real consequences. If your warehouse robot misses an object because it calculated the grasp angle wrong by a millimeter, that's money lost and work delayed. If an autonomous vehicle misreads the road, someone could get hurt. Physical systems also can't just 'retry' cheaply: every failure costs time, money, and safety. The robot has to be reliable from the start, and every problem the robot encounters in the real world needs to be fixed quickly—not weeks later when engineers finally analyze the data.

Scaling Robot Deployment

A robot pilot at one customer site looks like a win. The customer's operations team is happy. The ROI looks good. But that success hides the real cost of field deployment: the dedicated engineer living on-site for six months customizing everything, the layout tweaks, the tool changes, the safety sign-offs, the integration with their existing software systems. When you try to replicate the same robot at a second customer, nothing is the same. The bins are in a different location. They want the robot to run at a different speed. The floor is too oily for traction. The customer's IT team needs the robot to talk to their systems in a way you didn't anticipate. A startup's burn rate goes up faster than revenue because you're running 100 simultaneous pilots, each needing customization, each with misaligned expectations, and each revealing 5 to 10 unexpected problems.

Language To Action

In a factory or warehouse, humans can pick up a cardboard box wrapped in plastic and pull out what's inside without tearing it. A robot using just language and pattern-matching from videos often fails: it tears the plastic in a way that breaks its gripper, or throws the whole box away, or gets stuck because the plastic didn't move the way the model expected. The factory worker's target is maybe 2 minutes per box. The robot is slower, less reliable, and keeps asking a human to step in.

Robot Data And Learning

Robots don't have a free source of data the way language models do. Language models had the entire internet written for free. Self-driving cars get data from millions of drivers on the road every day—those drivers collect data for Tesla without being asked. But robots have neither. You can't build a warehouse of robots, switch them on, and let them collect data while working. You have to actively control them (through teleoperation: a person in a VR headset controlling the robot's hand), build fake training worlds in simulation, or hire humans to label videos. Data is expensive and slow to gather.

Robot Hardware And Components

You can buy commodity motors online and assemble robots from kits. But buying cheap parts does not guarantee a working robot. A weak gripper cannot feel what it is holding and cannot adjust grip strength, so it drops delicate items. A low-resolution camera cannot read dials or text, so inspection tasks fail even if the AI is smart. A compute system limited by battery power and heat cannot run perception and decision-making fast enough; by the time the robot decides to move, the moment has passed. The naive belief—that hardware is solved and only software matters—leads teams into dead ends.

Robot Business Models

A robot that works flawlessly in your lab may fail catastrophically on a customer's factory floor. One customer's e-commerce warehouse has bins spaced 1.5 meters apart; another has oily floors that make robots slip like ice skating. Your engineering team cannot predict every edge case—a flapping trash-can lid triggered a safety system, a conveyor's vibrations broke an assembly process, and an industrial fan's air pressure confused a perception system. Each customer has different people, different union rules, different IT systems, different production rhythms. Building a 'general-purpose' robot does not make it work in real life.

Robot Safety And Trust

A typical industrial robot is bolted to the floor or ceiling in a marked-off area, so if it fails, maybe it breaks some equipment. A humanoid can climb stairs, open doors, and walk anywhere. Physical damage to people is now possible. Testing software a million times can't catch every edge case. Meanwhile, hackers can compromise the robot over the network, a malicious person can put a fake "stop sign" sticker on the floor to fool the vision system, or data poisoning can change what the AI does. In traditional software, a bug loses some money. In robotics, a bug can injure someone, collapse investor confidence, and trigger regulators to lock down the entire sector with expensive rules.

Agentic AI Workflows

When you give a robot or software system a task, 'reasoning well' is not the same as 'succeeding in the world.' A language model can write perfect instructions, but if the robot never checks whether the instruction actually worked, it will pile up errors. Factory robots hit dropped parts, grippers drift out of alignment, or lighting changes and the camera sees something different. Without feedback, the robot keeps making the same mistake over and over. The system also has no sense of cost (how many retries before we lose money?), time pressure (the assembly line stops if we stall), or safety (can we touch that operator?). So the real problem: how do you build a system that acts, observes the outcome, and learns from failures before they cascade into lost productivity or safety hazards?
